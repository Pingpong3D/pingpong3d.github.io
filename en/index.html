Abstract

Pingpong3D turns an ordinary table tennis video into a match you can view from any angle. It needs only one camera: a TV broadcast, or a phone set up by the court. Using the table as a ruler, it calibrates the camera on every frame, rebuilds both players' full-body motion and the flight of the ball, then splits the match into points and strokes: who served, and where every ball landed.

On top of that, professional matches become a searchable rally library, and your own strokes can be lined up against a pro's at the moment of contact, with plain advice on what to fix first and by how much. Where the footage cannot support a measurement, it says “not judged” instead of guessing.

How it works

Everything comes from one camera view, in five steps.

  1. 01

    The table is the ruler

    Every frame re-calibrates the camera from the table edges, so it keeps up with broadcast pans.

    2.74 × 1.525 m · median edge error 0.49 px
  2. 02

    Both players in 3D

    NLF rebuilds the full body on every frame, with the racket arm fine-tuned for motion blur. Bodies are placed in a metric scene by the table, and limbs that jump for a frame or two are repaired.

    SMPL mesh · 41 keypoints
  3. 03

    The ball and every sound

    It finds the ball through motion blur, times each hit and bounce from the audio, and fits a physical flight to locate the landing.

    ball Ø 40 mm
  4. 04

    Rally library

    The match is split into points and strokes: who served, which stroke, where it landed. Pro matches can be searched on all of these.

    Paris QF · 123 rallies
  5. 05

    Advice you can act on

    Match analysis, tactics review, power guide, stroke comparison and self-review, in Chinese or English.

    中文 / English

What it won't guess

A single camera can't see everything. These stay out of the numbers:

Landing spots it can't see not judgedmarked as such on the page
Racket face, the wrist during motion blur not judgedstill under research
Ball spin not judged
Leg joint torques Not computed: they need ground-reaction forces that video can't provide. Only knee angles and the direction energy flows into the upper body.
Timing order at 30 fps Reference only; leg timing is given at 60 fps and above.
